I. AMPLITUDE & CALIBRATION

Question: Are the documents in proper amplitude?

Verdict: Yes — amplitude is properly standardised and calibrated.

The tracing is acquired and rendered at the internationally accepted diagnostic standard of 10 mm per millivolt on the vertical axis and 25 mm per second on the horizontal axis. A standardisation pulse is present at the left margin of each lead row and is visible across the rendered output. The underlying digital recording is acquired through a 12-bit analogue-to-digital converter over an input range of ±4 mV, yielding a least-significant-bit resolution of approximately 1.95 microvolts — well below the noise floor of a clinical-grade ECG. The signal chain incorporates band-pass filtering of 0.05–150 Hz with a 50 Hz notch (Butterworth, fourth-order), which is the configuration recommended by the American Heart Association / American College of Cardiology / Heart Rhythm Society Recommendations for the Standardisation and Interpretation of the Electrocardiogram.

On lead-by-lead amplitude verification of the raw signal, the peak-to-peak excursion ranges from 0.31 mV (lead aVL) to 1.68 mV (lead V2). All twelve leads sit comfortably within the ADC dynamic range; there is no evidence of waveform clipping, no railing against the ±4 mV limit, and no saturation artefact. III. LENGTH OF DATA

Question: What is the length of the data?

The audit on this parameter must distinguish three distinct measures of "length", because the question carries a different answer in each register. The three measures are stated separately:

1. Length of the rendered document. The rendered tracing (PNG) is a single page in landscape orientation, displaying the conventional 4 × 3 lead montage with a continuous rhythm strip in lead II at the foot of the page.

2. Length of the underlying digital signal. The raw recording, as disclosed in the accompanying JSON, is 13.038 seconds in duration. At a sampling rate of 500 Hz this comprises 6,519 samples per lead. Verification by direct inspection of the lead arrays confirms that all twelve leads carry exactly 6,519 samples with no dropped or missing data points, and that the duration computes correctly as 6,519 ÷ 500 = 13.038 seconds.

3. Diagnostic adequacy of length. The international convention for a diagnostic 12-lead ECG is a recording of approximately 10 seconds (typically 2.5 seconds displayed per lead in the four-column montage, with a longer rhythm strip in a chosen lead). The present recording exceeds this minimum by approximately 30 per cent, which is well within the range of clinical adequacy and permits both rhythm analysis and morphological interpretation. The duration is sufficient to (a) confirm the underlying rhythm; (b) compute reliable interval measurements; and (c) demonstrate the consistency of ST-segment morphology across multiple consecutive beats.

In short: the document is, on this parameter, of adequate length. There is no deficiency of duration that would impede diagnostic interpretation.

IV. DIAGNOSTIC DETECTABILITY OF DISEASE

Question: Can a disease be detected on the basis of the attached documents?

Verdict: Yes. The tracing demonstrates an unambiguous, life-threatening diagnosis — namely, Acute ST-Elevation Myocardial Infarction (STEMI) of the Anterior Wall, in evolution, most consistent with acute occlusion of the proximal-to-mid Left Anterior Descending (LAD) coronary artery.

The disease is detected on the strength of the following diagnostic criteria, each of which is independently met on the face of the present record and is, in conjunction, virtually pathognomonic:

It bears emphasis that the diagnosis is not borderline, not equivocal, and not dependent on subtle waveform analysis. The ST-segment elevation of 6.3 mm in lead V2 — over six times the diagnostic threshold of 1 mm — together with the typical convex-upwards ("tombstone") morphology, the contiguous involvement of four precordial leads, the corroborative reciprocal ST-depression in the inferior leads, and the early Q-wave formation, jointly constitute a textbook presentation. A second cardiologist re-reading the tracing would, in this reviewer's confident opinion, return the same diagnosis.

The clinical implication of this finding is that the patient was, at the moment of acquisition, suffering acute transmural myocardial injury and required immediate reperfusion therapy. Treatment recommendations have been set out in a separate clinical report and are not repeated here, the present document being confined to a quality-and-fitness audit of the tracing.

V. AUDIT CONCLUSION

On a careful examination of the attached electrocardiographic record on all four parameters posed for audit, the considered opinion of the undersigned is as follows:

(a). On amplitude — the document is properly calibrated and amplitude-standardised, with no evidence of clipping, saturation or under-gain.

(b). On labelling — the document is substantially and correctly labelled; the only omissions concern the reporting physician's and technician's identities, which are matters of authentication rather than diagnosis.

(c). On length — the document carries 13.038 seconds of 500 Hz, twelve-lead data (6,519 samples per lead), which exceeds the international diagnostic minimum and is adequate in every clinical respect.

(d). On disease detectability — yes; an Acute Anterior-Wall STEMI is unambiguously and confidently diagnosable from the tracing as presented.

The tracing is, accordingly, fit for diagnostic use and ought to attract significant evidentiary weight, subject only to the addition of the reporting physician's signed endorsement where the document is to be tendered for medico-legal or insurance purposes.
